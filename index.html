Summation

We are building a system that sends a current through the skin and extracts the response at several frequencies. The response is called an impedance spectrum and it holds information about the structure of the tissue underneath.

ESE 4970 Capstone Design, Washington University in St. Louis, Fall 2026

Justification

Currently, suspicious lesions are commonly evaluated through excision biopsy, which provides tissue for histological diagnosis. However, these procedures are invasive and can require extensive planning and post-procedure wound care to reduce risks such as bleeding and infection.

EIS provides a noninvasive method for quantitatively evaluating tissue electrical properties and could provide additional information when evaluating potentially abnormal lesions. This is particularly significant for melanoma because when detected early, the five-year survival rate is more than 99%. Also, existing systems are not commericially available and are very expensive.

Our goal is to build an accessible, low-cost hardware make reliable quantitative skin measurements. Our prototype is not a diagnostic tool but rather a proof of concept and a foundation for future work on affordable tissue assessment.

Analog hardware    Digital processing on the PYNQ-Z2

1. Generate the signal

The FPGA generates a digital wave and the samples go to a 12-bit DAC. During early testing, a bench function generator is used so we can verify the circuit first.

2. Apply it safely and measure the response

A current-limiting resistor and a DC-blocking capacitor control the signal before it reaches the skin. Two electrodes inject current while the other two measure voltage. A precision reference resistor in the loop tells us how much current is flowing.

3. Find magnitude and phase on the FPGA

The FPGA multiplies the digitized voltage and current by reference sine and cosine waves (I/Q demodulation). This pulls out how large each signal is and how far it is shifted in time.

4. Compute impedance and fit the model

The ARM processor computes the complex impedance at each frequency, corrects for circuit effects using a known resistor, and fits the Cole-Cole model. The model reduces the whole spectrum to four numbers: low- and high-frequency resistance, a time constant, and a spread factor.

5. Show the results

After each sweep, the board sends the spectrum and fitted parameters over Wi-Fi to a Flask dashboard in the browser.

Hardware

  • PYNQ-Z2 board with a Zynq FPGA and ARM processor
  • Pmod DA2 12-bit DAC and Pmod AD1 12-bit, 1 MSPS ADC
  • Two INA128 instrumentation amplifiers, gain of about 10
  • OPA2134 buffering a 1.65 V midpoint so signals stay in the ADC range
  • BAT85 diodes clamping ADC inputs between 0 and 3.3 V
  • Two 9 V batteries for an isolated ±9 V supply

Safety first. Our current limits follow published skin-impedance work and sit at or below the IEC 60601-1 patient auxiliary current limits. Every frequency is checked on a 100 Ω test load with an oscilloscope before any skin contact, and we test only on ourselves as healthy volunteers. The device is battery powered and is not a certified medical device.
