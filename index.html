PYNQ-Z2-Based Electrical Impedance Spectroscopy for Skin Tissue Characterization

Scope

Project Scope

We are building a system that sends a current through the skin and extracts the response at several frequencies. The result is an impedance spectrum and it gives information about the structure of the tissue underneath.

Why

Justification

Currently, suspicious lesions are commonly evaluated through excision biopsy, which provides tissue for histological diagnosis [1]. However, these procedures are invasive and can require extensive planning and post-procedure wound care to reduce risks such as bleeding and infection [2].

EIS provides a noninvasive method for quantitatively evaluating tissue electrical properties and could provide additional information when evaluating potentially abnormal lesions. This is particularly significant for melanoma because when detected early, the five-year survival rate is more than 99% [3]. Also, existing systems are not commercially available and are very expensive [4].

Our goal is to build an accessible, low-cost hardware that makes reliable quantitative skin measurements. Our prototype is not a diagnostic tool but rather a proof of concept and a foundation for future work on affordable tissue assessment.

System

Project Description

Our system uses the FPGA on a PYNQ-Z2 board to generate sine waves at 20 or more frequencies between 1 kHz and 100 kHz. A 12-bit DAC turns each wave into a small AC signal, and a front-end circuit limits the current before it reaches the skin. Four electrodes are placed on the skin: the outer two inject the current and the inner two measure the resulting voltage.

The response is digitized and captured by the FPGA. The ARM processor on the same chip fits each signal to a sine and cosine to find how large it is and how far it is shifted in time, turns those numbers into a complex impedance at each frequency, corrects for circuit effects, and fits the Cole-Cole tissue model. The resulting spectrum and four fitted parameters are plotted in a Jupyter notebook on the board after every sweep.

Aims

Aims

Measurable targets for our finished system

Signal generation

The FPGA sends sine waves to the test sample and receives the response from the electrodes.

  • At least 20 frequencies from 1 kHz to 100 kHz
  • Each within 1% of its setpoint

Impedance analysis

The ARM computes impedance, fits Cole-Cole parameters, and plots the results.

  • Magnitude within 5% and phase within 3° of a reference instrument, on precision resistors and an RC tissue phantom
  • Fitted phantom Cole-Cole parameters within 10% of the values predicted from its components

Skin measurements

The full system measures skin across the sweep and compares the result to published data.

  • Repeated measurements at the same site agree within a 10% coefficient of variation
  • The spectrum shows the frequency dependence reported in the literature
  • A full sweep and display update take at most 60 seconds

Design

Hardware

The PYNQ-Z2's Zynq chip combines programmable logic (the FPGA), which generates and captures the signals, with a dual-core ARM processor that runs Python and Jupyter.

Turns the FPGA’s digital sine samples into a 2 Vpp analog wave that drives the front end.

Samples both measurement channels at the same time and sends them back to the FPGA.

One measures the voltage across the inner sense electrodes; the other measures the current through a 1 kΩ reference resistor.

Holds both amplifier outputs at the middle of the ADC’s 0–3.3 V range so the whole sine wave can be measured.

Clamp the ADC inputs between 0 and 3.3 V so a fault can’t damage the Pmod or the board.

Power the analog front end without any connection to wall power.

Electrodes

We use four adhesive ECG electrode pads. The outer two send a small current through the tissue, and the inner two measure the voltage it creates.

Software

The FPGA generates the sine wave and captures the response. The ARM computes the impedance and plots the results.

A DDS sine generator sets each sweep frequency and streams samples to the Pmod DA2 over SPI. On the way back, it reads the Pmod AD1 and captures both channels.

Fits each captured channel to a sine and cosine at the excitation frequency to get its in-phase and quadrature parts, computes the complex impedance, corrects it with a sweep on a known resistor, and fits the Cole-Cole model to the full spectrum.

Runs in a notebook on the board. After each sweep it shows the waveforms, the impedance spectrum, and the Cole-Cole fit, and saves the results so measurements can be compared later.

Safety

64 µA

Worst-case excitation current (rms), with the electrodes shorted. This is below the IEC 60601-1 patient auxiliary current limit of 100 µA.

±9 V

The front end runs on two batteries so there is no connection to wall power.

Testing

We tested on a mock skin circuit and on bananas. The device is a prototype and not a certified medical device.

Results

We tested the system on three samples: a mock skin circuit with known values, a healthy banana, and a bruised banana.

What we tested

Mock skin

A resistor and capacitor circuit that behaves like skin. We know its exact values, so it shows how accurate the system is.

Healthy banana

Real biological tissue with intact cells.

Bruised banana

The same kind of tissue with damaged cells, standing in for unhealthy tissue.

Why bananas instead of skin

A banana lets us compare healthy and damaged tissue, which we can’t safely do on a person.

We checked that the system works on our own forearm, but we don’t report that data, because our device is a prototype and not a certified medical device.

A banana makes a better experiment because we can damage it on purpose. Like skin, banana tissue is made of cells whose membranes separate conductive fluid inside and outside the cell. That structure is what makes tissue impedance change with frequency, and it is what the Cole–Cole model describes [6].

Bruising breaks cells, so low-frequency current can reach more of the tissue. The impedance drops and the Cole–Cole curve shrinks [7]. Decaying banana tissue shows the same pattern: lower resistance and a higher characteristic frequency [8].

That makes a bruised banana a simple stand-in for unhealthy tissue. In skin, changes to the tissue from disease also change its impedance, which is how impedance measurements can help tell melanoma apart from harmless moles [9].

How we get the numbers

Open any step to see the math.

The FPGA captures the voltage on both channels. On the ARM, each channel is fit to a sine and a cosine at the excitation frequency (plus an offset and a slow drift) by least squares. The two fitted coefficients are the in-phase (I) and quadrature (Q) parts.

amplitude = √(I² + Q²)
phase = atan2(Q, I)

One amplifier measures the voltage across the inner electrodes. The other measures the voltage across a 1 kΩ resistor in series with the sample, which tells us the current. After dividing out the amplifier gain (≈ 9.9):

current = VR7 / 1 kΩ
|Z| = Velectrodes / current
phase of Z = phaseelectrodes − phasecurrent

The amplifiers and filters add a small gain and phase shift of their own that changes with frequency. We sweep a known resistor once, measure how far off each frequency is, and divide that out of every later measurement.

The Cole–Cole model describes tissue with four parameters. The fit adjusts them until the model curve matches the measured points as closely as possible (weighted complex least squares).

Z(f) = R∞ + (R0 − R∞) / (1 + (j·f/fc)α)

R0 is the resistance at very low frequency, when current can only flow around the cells. R∞ is the resistance at very high frequency, when current also passes through the cell membranes. fc is where the change between them is fastest, and α describes how spread out that change is.

For a resistor Rs in series with a resistor Rp and capacitor C in parallel, the expected values follow directly from the parts. The page computes these automatically from the values listed under Test 1.

R0 = Rs + Rp
R∞ = Rs
fc = 1 / (2π · Rp · C)
α = 1
Z(f) = Rs + Rp / (1 + j·2πf·Rp·C)

percent error = |measured − expected| / expected × 100%
phase error = measured − expected (degrees)
percent difference = (bruised − healthy) / healthy × 100%
